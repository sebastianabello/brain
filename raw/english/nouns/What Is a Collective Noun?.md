
A **collective noun** is a [noun](https://www.scribbr.com/category/nouns-and-pronouns/) that refers to some sort of group or collective—of people, animals, things, etc. Collective nouns are normally not treated as [plural](https://www.scribbr.com/nouns-and-pronouns/plural-noun/), even though they refer to a group of something.

Collective nouns include [common nouns](https://www.scribbr.com/nouns-and-pronouns/common-nouns/) like “group” and [proper nouns](https://www.scribbr.com/nouns-and-pronouns/proper-nouns/) like “Google” or “The Rolling Stones.”

Examples: Collective nouns in a sentence

The **party** will have to rethink its strategy to win the next election.

A **flock** of [sheep](https://www.scribbr.com/plurals/plural-of-sheep/) is crossing the road, delaying motorists.

**The Beatles** broke up when Paul McCartney quit the **band**.

## Are collective nouns singular or plural?

Collective nouns are most commonly treated as singular (i.e., used with [singular verb forms](https://www.scribbr.com/verbs/subject-verb-agreement/) like “is”), but usage varies between [US and UK English](https://www.scribbr.com/category/us-vs-uk/):

- In **US English**, it’s standard to always treat collective nouns as singular.
- In **UK English**, either way is acceptable, and usage tends to vary depending on the context.

### US English: Always singular

In US English, it’s regarded as incorrect to use a plural [verb](https://www.scribbr.com/category/verbs/) form with a collective noun. This doesn’t mean that people never do so, but it would be against the rules of most American style guides.

In [formal and academic writing](https://www.scribbr.com/category/academic-writing/), therefore, you should be careful to treat collective nouns as singular, even when they are plural in appearance (e.g., some band names).

Examples: Collective nouns in US English

The **board** is in disagreement about appointing a successor.

**Foo Fighters** is an overrated **band**.

### UK English: Context-dependent

In UK English, usage is more varied, and both styles are generally seen as acceptable. In [practice](https://www.scribbr.com/us-vs-uk/practise-or-practice/), the plural form tends to be used quite often when the emphasis is on the individual members of a group acting separately, or when the noun is plural in appearance.

It’s still a good idea to be consistent about how you treat each term, though. For example, it would read strangely to treat the word “company” as singular in one sentence and plural in the next.

Examples: Collective nouns in UK English

The **board** are in disagreement about appointing a successor.

**Foo Fighters** are an overrated **band**, but **Nirvana** is great.

## Collective nouns for animals

Many collective nouns are very general in their usage (e.g., you can refer to a “group” of just about anything), but there are also much more specific collective nouns, particularly for different types of animals. These terms are used on their own or with the animal specified (e.g., “pack of dogs”).

These nouns range from the conventional to the poetic and unusual. The more surprising ones tend not to be used very often in practice, except to comment on their strangeness. Some examples are shown in the table below.

|Collective noun|Animals it’s used for|
|---|---|
|Brood|Baby animals such as chicks or insect larvae|
|Colony|Social and cooperative animals (e.g., ants, beavers, penguins)|
|Flock|Animals guided by a leader (e.g., sheep following a shepherd) or assembled in a formation (e.g., birds)|
|Gaggle|Geese, and sometimes other birds|
|Herd|Animals (e.g., cows) kept by farmers in a large group, or wild animals that congregate in groups|
|Murder|Crows|
|Murmuration|Starlings|
|Pack|Wild or trained animals that hunt as a group (e.g., wolves, dogs)|
|Parliament|Owls, and sometimes other birds|
|Plague|Locusts|
|Shrewdness|Apes|
|Unkindness|Ravens|

Note

The more common collective nouns for animals are often applied figuratively to humans, typically with a negative connotation. For example, referring to a group of people as a “herd” would characterize them as mindless and anonymous.
